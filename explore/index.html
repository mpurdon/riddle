The verdict

Riddle's engine is honest and reversible. Its surface hides both.

The audit found a product whose foundations are unusually good: one shortcut table drives keys and help, every disabled control says why, every automated change lands in an Activity ledger with undo, and the per-button help table computes what a click does to Gmail for the account in view. Reviewers of other clients single out exactly these properties as the basis of trust, and Riddle already has them.

What the surface does is bury them. A first-run user is told "Nothing needs your attention" with no account connected. The plain-English reason a Find exists is computed and never rendered. Nothing at the moment of acting says whether Gmail changed. The best explanation of consequences in the app is behind a small "?" button. And a new user meets about 45 domain nouns, 16 of them before the daily loop makes sense.

What to keep

The foundations other clients lack

  • One shortcut table drives keys and help, and one action-help table drives the per-row "?" and the help sheet. The "?" dialog's Riddle / Gmail / Teaches / Undo columns, computed for the account in view, are the best explanation of consequences in any mail client we studied.
  • Honest status everywhere. "Catching up" instead of a false Attention Zero; progress that moves only on real events; "Nothing is hidden" on every degraded path; "Not sifted yet" instead of a fake explanation.
  • The account model. Option cards with consequences, the "Right now:" sentence, "Held because…", Observe and None as defaults, Trusted behind a hold with coaching, inline type-the-count removal, groups locked while rules use them.
  • Confirmation tiers are structural and re-validated in the Action layer: real recipients, the exact count, a hold with keyboard support, an approval that shows real arguments.
  • Undo is real. Toast Undo, global u, Activity Undo and Undo All with skipped lists, undone entries struck through, "Cannot undo: sent mail cannot be unsent."
  • Activity reads like the README promised: actor chips, "Triggered by Mesh: … v7" as a link, why lines, View Messages, Edit Mesh, View Find.
  • Explanations exist for every decision. The route walk with actual values, the LLM rationale as a quote, "Shadow: proposed, not applied". The content is there; it needs a plain-English layer on top.
  • Security-minded mail rendering: sandboxed bodies, remote images off with the reason stated, links neutralised and listed, "auth failed" and Reply-To mismatch chips, a Contamination note that says what is disabled.
  • Director transparency: tool-call cards with latency and an Activity link, per-turn cost, the untrusted-content banner, proposals previewed on real arguments.
The vocabulary problem

Where the archaeology metaphor helps and where it hides meaning

Jared Spool's model of intuitive design puts every user at a "current knowledge" point. For a Riddle newcomer that point is Gmail's words: label, archive, spam. What mail clients teach

Seventeen clients, one lesson: show the reason, scope the correction, never destroy

Only two clients in the set show a per-message "why": Gmail's importance marker (hover for the reason since December 2010, click to correct and train) and Outlook's Copilot "Prioritize my inbox", whose headline replaces the preview line and which Microsoft's own rollout found was the thing users valued. Outlook's older Focused Inbox is the counter-example: no explanation, an override list the user cannot see, and institutions publishing "turn it off" guides after mail was missed.

Patterns that consistently work

  1. Sender-scoped corrections that persist and are visible as rules.
  2. The reason on the row.
  3. Undo as a toast on every automated or bulk move.
  4. Nothing destroyed by triage: Done, not delete; recoverable screening.
  5. AI drafts, humans send.
  6. Propose, then confirm, for bulk actions.
  7. Preview matches before a rule goes live.
  8. A stable keyboard vocabulary: j/k, e, h, l, /, ⌘K.
  9. Few buckets, whole counts: 3 to 7 streams.
  10. Quiet by default; notify only the attention tier.
  11. Time-sensitive overrides the category.
  12. A digest for the non-urgent tier.
  13. Start clean, on real mail, reversibly.

Failures on the record

  1. Hidden overrides and no "why" (Focused Inbox).
  2. Auto-archiving on a classifier the user cannot inspect (Superhuman Auto Archive, Fyxer's FYI).
  3. Bundles that override the user's system (Inbox by Gmail, retired, never ported).
  4. Forced re-screening of every sender (HEY).
  5. Auto-labels you cannot edit or test (Notion Mail); fixed taxonomies (Fyxer).
  6. AI that slows the core loop (Superhuman, Shortwave complaints).
  7. Default-on summaries with known error rates (Gemini, Apple).
  8. "Learns from you" without evidence (Fyxer, Focused).
  9. The inbox stops being opened (Notion Mail's stated reason for shutting down). A triage app must stay the place where decisions are reviewed, or it dies.
What communication tools teach

Reason on the row, three exits, two lists, allow-lists above inference

GitHub's notification inbox prints a reason label on every row (mention, review requested, subscribed), makes it a filter, and ships it as an email header. Linear's Inbox has a Priority tab that separates "needs you" from other updates; its snooze ends at the chosen time or on new activity, whichever comes first. Slack's VIPs and Apple's Focus allow-lists sit above every inference and bypass quiet modes. Android lets an app propose a notification's importance but only the user can raise it.

Ten transferable lessons

  1. Put the reason on the row and make it a filter and a correction.
  2. Two lists beat one ranking: a wrong call lands in the other list, visibly.
  3. User allow-lists sit above all inference and quiet modes. That is Never Hidden; expose it with one-key add.
  4. Snooze returns on new activity, not only on the clock.
  5. Done is a state with a retention window and an undo, never a delete.
  6. Three exits: handled, later, stop telling me. A single "dismiss" loses the signal.
  7. Single-key vocabulary with a palette that prints the keys.
  8. Latency is a feature and comes from local-first data.
  9. AI output is labelled, per-source disable-able and pausable.
  10. Digest what is still unread; adapt to presence without broadcasting it.

The cost of interruption, measured

  • Pielot, Church and de Oliveira (MobileHCI 2014): 63.5 notifications a day, viewed within minutes even on silent; more notifications, more negative emotion.
  • Fournier et al.: a single notification slows processing by about 7 seconds (d = 0.62); participants received over 150 a day.
  • Microsoft's 2025 telemetry for the top fifth of users: an interruption every two minutes, 275 a day.
  • Slack's own design team on threads: hiding replies from the channel was "the single most meaningful change"; unlimited nesting made the design "extremely complex". Coding Horror argues for one level at most. Riddle's new reply tree should keep collapse controls and a depth cap.
What the research says

Email behaviour, attention, and trust in automation

The research review covered about sixty studies. The findings below are the ones that change what Riddle should do, with the strength of the evidence stated. Two popular ideas did not survive: decision fatigue (the hungry-judge and ego-depletion effects are contested; a 23-lab replication found d ≈ 0.04) and choice overload as a general law (a meta-analysis of 50 experiments found a mean effect near zero). What replicates is interruption cost and bounded working memory.

How people actually use email

  • Half of mail is left in the inbox. Dabbish et al. (CHI 2005): of 581 rated messages, 24 percent deleted, 27 percent filed, 49 percent left; 19 checks a day. Whittaker and Sidner's 1996 filers, no-filers and spring cleaners still describe people.
  • Cutting email off halves task switching. Mark, Voida and Cardello (CHI 2012): window switches fell from 37 to 18 an hour and dwell time nearly doubled over five days without email. Small sample, one site.
  • 77 checks a day, and checking correlates with stress. Mark et al. (CHI 2016), 40 workers over 12 days. Notification-driven checkers rated their productivity lower than self-interrupting ones. Batching raised rated productivity but there was no evidence it lowered stress.
  • Three checks a day lowers stress. Kushlev and Dunn (2015), within-subjects: checks fell from 12.5 to 4.7 with the same volume handled. Fitz et al. (2019), a randomised trial of 237: three notification batches a day beat hourly, which equalled no change, and no notifications at all raised anxiety.
  • Gmail's Priority Inbox saved 6 percent of reading time and 13 percent on unimportant mail, with corrections as the training signal (Aberdeen et al., 2010).
  • Interruptions cost stress, not time. Mark, Gudith and Klocke (CHI 2008): interrupted people finished faster with equal quality but reported more workload and frustration. The "23 minutes to recover" figure is not in that paper.

Trust in automation and AI

  • Explanations raise acceptance whether the AI is right or wrong. Bansal et al. (CHI 2021), three tasks, large N: explanations added no accuracy. Confidence scores calibrate trust; explanations do not. Zhang, Liao and Bellamy (2020).
  • Human plus AI teams lose on decision tasks by default. Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone (Nature Human Behaviour 2024), 106 experiments: g = −0.23 overall, −0.27 on decisions; the team wins only when the human is the stronger judge, which is exactly the borderline case.
  • One visible error costs an algorithm more trust than a human's (Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey 2015), and letting people adjust the output even slightly restores use (Dietvorst et al. 2018).
  • Explanatory debugging (Kulesza, Burnett, Wong and Stumpf, IUI 2015) is the closest study to editing Riddle's rules: a mail classifier that explains each prediction and lets users edit the explanation. Principles: be sound, be complete, do not overwhelm (ten features did as well as fifty); be actionable, be reversible, always honour feedback, change incrementally. Mental-model scores 52 percent higher; corrections up to twice as efficient. Users' top question: "what happens if I change this?"
  • Cognitive forcing (Buçinca et al. 2021): showing the AI's verdict on demand, after the user decides, or after a delay cut overreliance, at a satisfaction cost.
  • Amershi et al.'s 18 guidelines (CHI 2019) name the checklist: make clear what the system can do and how well; support efficient dismissal and correction; make clear why it did what it did; convey the consequences of user actions; provide global controls; notify about changes.
  • Prefer undo to confirmation (Nielsen 2018; Shneiderman's rule 6): confirmations habituate; a generic "Are you sure?" protects nothing; name the action on the button.
  • Jargon hurts even with definitions (Shulman et al. 2020, N = 650); bad metaphors slow learning (Carroll and Thomas 1982; Gentner and Nielsen 1996). Tours are skipped and forgotten; contextual, pull-based help works (NN/g 2023). Visible work is valued (Buell and Norton 2011) and people value rules they finished themselves (Norton, Mochon and Ariely 2012).

What that means for Riddle

  • Lead a Find with its confidence and the rule that fired, not a persuasive narrative. Never show a confidence the model cannot back.
  • Treat Unresolved as the primary human task, not an error bucket, with at most four decisions on screen and each resolution a rule-teaching moment.
  • Build the correction loop as explanatory debugging: show what fired, let the user edit it, re-classify at once, show the delta, keep it undoable, and never silently discard a correction.
  • In Spoil and Contamination, the views that can hide mail, show the message before Riddle's verdict; in Finds, verdict first.
  • Make the autonomy rung per rule, promote on evidence (N approvals, zero reversals), demote on a reversal, and show per-rule precision ("reversed 3 of 40 times").
  • Batch write-backs and notifications to a few moments a day; a count of decisions pending, not messages, is the only ambient signal; allow-listed senders bypass.
  • Close every review with what was resolved and learned; show partial progress Riddle already made.
  • Keep the metaphor only with a plain gloss on every term, Gmail's own verbs for actions, and no metaphor words in errors or confirmations.

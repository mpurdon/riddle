Luke Wroblewski

The designer who named Mobile First, measured what hiding navigation costs, and now runs a live AI product on his own writing. His argument for Riddle: show what the system can do, start with an answer, and put the person at review rather than at a prompt.

Major ideas

Thirteen ideas, each with its evidence

Wroblewski argues from measurement: eye-tracking studies, engagement deltas after a redesign, usage logs from his own products. Every idea below names the study or the product it came from.

2009 · 2011

Mobile First: constraints as clarity

A 320 by 480 screen has no room for "extraneous, unnecessary elements", so designing for it first forces a team to agree on what matters most. His question: if you can agree on the most important content and features for a small screen, why should that priority change when there is more room? The book turns this into a discipline with chapters on Organization, Actions, Inputs and Layout.

Example. The talk ran at 50 events from An Event Apart Seattle (April 2010) to 2013, and the A Book Apart book followed in October 2011.
Riddle's dense single window carries 45 domain nouns. Mobile First asks which six a person needs in the first minute.
2015

Obvious always wins

When Polar replaced its segmented control with a toggle menu, people stopped moving between the app's sections. Zeebox saw the same drop after moving to a navigation drawer; Facebook and Redbooth saw engagement rise after moving from a hamburger to a bottom tab bar. Navigation is how an app shows what is possible. Hide it and people do not know what they can do.

Example. The 2015 article became a talk delivered nine times in 2017 and 2018, including An Event Apart Denver.
Keep the rail visible with counts. Never collapse it to icons. Shrink type before hiding labels.
2007 · 2008

Form design from eye-tracking

With the London firm Etre he measured label placement and button layout. Top-aligned labels are captured with the input in one eye movement and complete fastest; left-aligned labels slow people down, which is right for unfamiliar data. Reset, Cancel and Go Back are secondary actions; a Cancel placed on the right cost about six seconds and six participants clicked it by mistake.

Example. "Align primary actions with input fields for a clear path to completion." Web Form Design, Rosenfeld Media, May 2008.
One primary button per wizard step, on the input's left edge. Cancel as a link, never a twin button.
2009

Inline validation, after the field

In a 22-participant study the best variant gave 22 percent more completions, 22 percent fewer errors, 31 percent higher satisfaction and 42 percent faster completion. The rules that produced it: validate when the person leaves the field, not per keystroke; validate only where errors are likely; keep success marks persistent.

Example. A List Apart, "Inline Validation in Web Forms", September 2009.
The Mesh editor is the high-error surface. Validate on blur and show the match count, so meaning is checked, not only syntax.
2008 · 2010

Gradual engagement

"Sign Up Forms Must Die": a registration form as the first step treats people as database records before they have a reason to care. Registration is "either postponed, or handled behind the scenes" and the first experience shows how the service is useful. Twitter added a step (pick topics you care about) and completions rose 29 percent.

Example. TripIt let people email an itinerary and get a result before any account existed.
Let people see Riddle sift a sample before the Google Cloud steps. Observe mode is the gradual-engagement path.
2012

One thumb, one eyeball

People check phones about 150 times a day in short, distracted, one-handed bursts. Polar set a litmus test: vote on ten polls or create one in under 60 seconds with one thumb. Experienced users did it in about 30. Quoting Dan Formosa: design for the extremes and the middle takes care of itself.

Example. The Mobile First book lists the modes: Lookup, Explore, Check In, Edit. Riddle's daily use is Check In.
A timed test: open Riddle, see what needs attention, dispose of one item, leave in under a minute, keyboard only.
2013

Input matters

His Designing for Touch talk and his notes on Josh Clark's work carry the guidance: a 7 mm target (about 44 px), content at the top and controls at the bottom on phones, and hover as an enhancement, never the only way to do something. Clarity beats density on every interface.

Example. The 44 px figure is Apple's guideline, relayed through his 2013 notes.
Riddle's 10 to 11 px chips and hover-only titles fail this test on a desktop too.
2011 – 2013

Data-informed, not data-decided

Forty-two "Data Monday" posts used sourced numbers to make design arguments: 3.6 million smartphones a day against 371 thousand births. He is careful about the limit: "If you're data informed instead, then you're still the one making a decision."

Example. The form studies count fixations and seconds; the AI posts count answers and study participants.
Riddle already records every decision. Show the numbers that justify a change before it is applied.
2024 · 2025

AI as a feature versus a product

A bolted-on AI feature competes with every existing feature for attention, and the numbers "look good" only because "even turkeys fly in hurricanes". Building from the ground up means earning each user by providing value. An embedded app inherits inputs and integrations but is limited by what its host allows.

Example. Microsoft Edge: AI icons beside shopping, games and Collections, and people tune everything out.
A "Director" rail item competes with Finds. Put proposals where the evidence is and keep chat small.
2024 – 2025

Chat's limits and intent-driven UI

People know how to use chat and can state intent directly, but "without clear affordances, people don't know what they can do", scrolling threads is painful, and language is poor at visual and spatial things. Single-pane chat gives way to split panes and then to agent products where chat "remains collapsed by default".

Example. "Chatting back and forth to create things feels dated now." The Receding Role of AI Chat, June 2025.
Plain-language rule requests that the Director turns into Mesh text, shown for approval, teach the DSL by refinement.
2025 · 2026

Durable patterns: suggestions, citations, less text

Three problems persist across every AI product: people do not know what it can do, what information it used, or how to digest long text. From Ask LukeW's usage: suggested questions "did an enormous amount of heavy lifting" and citations give "a real trust boost". A January 2025 study with designers confirmed all three.

Example. Participants were intimidated by long answers and asked for visuals; old content raised doubts.
Every proposal cites the messages that motivated it, with counts per disposition.
2025 · 2026

Show the agent's work, collapsed

Users split into two camps regardless of experience: some find an agent's process overwhelming, others need it to monitor. The answer is progressive disclosure: tool calls collapsed to one expandable summary line, each step linked to its result, and less visual weight as the tool count rises. Humans belong at review, approve, steer and delegate.

Example. The management checklist: start, schedule, monitor, redirect, stop, capture results. Inbox framing works for agents needing a decision.
Proposal cards: one summary line, an expander, per-step links to exact message lists.
2025 · 2026

Let the AI do the onboarding

Instead of tours and empty states, generate a first result and let people edit it: "AI does the creation, people do the curation." Nearly every chat product opens with a question, yet "people regularly struggle with knowing what to ask", so start with an answer. Forms follow: people communicate naturally and software handles the structure.

Example. A photographed concert poster becomes database fields. "No form is the best kind of Web Form Design."
The Director opens with what it noticed since last visit and two proposals, not an empty box.

Applied to Riddle

Thirteen implications for Riddle's flows

Each item names the idea it comes from. The flow tag says which part of Riddle it changes.

  1. 1
    NavigationKeep the rail fully visible with counts on every destination. Riddle's value is that Finds, Unresolved, Spoil and Contamination are distinct places to look; Polar lost section-to-section movement the day it hid its sections. Under density pressure, shrink type before hiding labels.From: Obvious always wins.
  2. 2
    OnboardingLet people watch Riddle work before asking for anything. On first launch, run the default Meshes over the fixture mailbox so a Finds list, an Unresolved list and an Activity ledger exist before Google Cloud is mentioned. Connecting Gmail in Observe mode, with nothing written back, is registration "handled behind the scenes".From: Gradual engagement; Let the AI do the onboarding.
  3. 3
    SettingsTop-aligned labels, one primary action per step, Cancel as a link. The wizard's data is familiar, so top-aligned labels are fastest. Use left-aligned labels or inline help only where the input is unfamiliar, such as a Mesh's target disposition. The six-second, six-misclick Cancel finding argues against symmetrical button pairs anywhere.From: Form design from eye-tracking.
  4. 4
    MeshesValidate the Mesh DSL on blur, and show the match count as feedback. Syntax errors are cheap to catch; meaning errors are what bite. A rule that matches zero messages, or three thousand, should say so before Apply. Do not inline-validate trivial fields such as a Dig's name.From: Inline validation, after the field.
  5. 5
    Daily loopMake the check-in glance a timed test. Open Riddle, see what needs attention, dispose of one item, leave in under a minute, keyboard only. Run it in the fixture suite. It also argues for Summary as the landing view rather than a full Inbox.From: One thumb, one eyeball.
  6. 6
    DirectorOpen the Director with an answer, not a prompt. A generated note of what it noticed since the last visit (an Unresolved cluster, a sender that keeps landing in Spoil, a Mesh that never fires) and two or three proposals to accept or dismiss. Suggested questions did the heavy lifting in his product; an empty box will not.From: Let the AI do the onboarding; Durable patterns.
  7. 7
    DirectorEvery proposal cites the messages that motivated it. A Mesh proposal lists the messages it would have matched, with hover previews and a "would have changed" count per disposition. Keep the observability rule: ids and counts, never subjects, in logs.From: Durable patterns; Show the agent's work.
  8. 8
    DirectorShow the Director's work collapsed, one line per step, each linked to its result. "Read 140 Unresolved messages, found 3 clusters" with an expander. As tools grow, reduce the trace's visual weight rather than its length.From: Show the agent's work, collapsed.
  9. 9
    AutonomyMake the autonomy rung visible per Dig, everywhere, with a one-key way to drop a rung. His management checklist is start, schedule, monitor, redirect, stop, capture. Riddle has capture (Activity) and stop (Undo). It lacks monitor: the rung is shown only in Settings. Add an inbox-style queue of proposals with a count.From: Show the agent's work; Obvious always wins.
  10. 10
    TrustVerify through the Activity ledger, not through extra confirmations. At Act and Trusted, write to Gmail without a dialog, but make each ledger row link to the rule that fired and the message, and keep Undo one keystroke away. He treats every extra secondary action as a cost.From: Show the agent's work; Form design.
  11. 11
    MeshesPlain language in, Mesh text out, shown for approval. "People don't know what AI can do nor how to guide it"; the same holds for a small IF/ELSE DSL. The rewrite stays visible and editable so people learn the DSL by observing then refining.From: Chat's limits and intent-driven UI.
  12. 12
    NavigationEmbed the Director in the triage views instead of a separate destination. Surface proposals inline where the evidence is: an Unresolved cluster header, a Spoil sender row. Keep chat collapsed to a small pane as the fallback for questions the inline proposals cannot answer.From: AI as a feature versus a product; Chat's limits.
  13. 13
    SettingsPublish Riddle's steering layer, read-only and versioned. The Director's prompt, the Never Hidden gate and the disposition taxonomy are a steering layer in his sense. Make them inspectable from Settings so behaviour is reviewed by people rather than tuned per session.From: Steering layers.

What we could not confirm

No talk titled "Conversational Interfaces" is in his presentations index. He has no Substack; his writing is on lukew.com. The 44 px touch-target figure is Apple's guideline relayed through his notes on Josh Clark's 2013 talk, not his own slide.

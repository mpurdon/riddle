Product design lead · co-host of Design Life · teacher of strategic influence

Femke van Schoonhoven

A decade of shipped work at Uber, Wealthsimple and Gusto, taught back to a six-figure audience for six years. Her thesis: designers stall not for lack of craft but because they cannot frame decisions in terms others evaluate. For Riddle her most useful material is the smaller part: how a control must mean one clear thing, how a "pending" state needs recovery, and why a complex screen that is clear beats a simple one that hides why nothing happened.

Major ideas

Twelve ideas, from her articles, newsletter and podcast

Where a point comes from a machine transcript of a video, the card says so; the wording there is not typeset-exact.

2018

A design decision must do three jobs

Solve a problem, be easy for users, and be supported by everyone. She admits she tends "to swing between number one and two". Her conversational techniques: "Repeat and Rephrase" (convert "likes" into "works"), "Come back to the big 3", and "Lead with a Yes". She also writes user stories for the internal team: "As an engineer I value technical constraints…"

Example. Documentation as "a single source of truth" that "avoids repeat conversations" when someone joins a project halfway.
An acceptance template for any UI change: which problem, how it is easier, why the team should support it.
2026

Pushback is a framing problem

"Pushback in design reviews is almost never actually a rationale problem. It's a framing problem." Translate rationale into the stakeholder's currency; frame the decision before showing anything visual, because "here's what I've been working on" invites everyone to react to pixels; pre-sell likely objectors; bring crisp questions; show rejected options. "Most designers only show their thinking once it's under attack. By then it reads as defense."

Example. "If you save it for the review, instead of getting feedback, you're getting reactions."
Riddle's own UI reviews: open with the decision being asked and the rejected variant as a screenshot.
2021

Document throughout, not at handoff

From her UXConf talk: alignment in review one, pushback in review two, because "some of the people in the room now weren't in the room earlier on". Her Figma template has an "about this project" page, a design deck with an "options compared" section recording why one direction won, an out-of-scope graveyard, saved alternative explorations, and handoff components carrying a status.

Example. "I've definitely had it where I've been challenged like a month later." (machine transcript)
A Director proposal should read as "options compared": the problem, the alternatives rejected, the blast radius.
2020

Design for people, not users; states that recover

Uber's cash collection in Mexico: drivers tapped briskly, reached the "pending" screen and assumed payment was processing, some phoning support at once. The fix was not to simplify but to add recovery: start over, cancel, and view the code from the pending screen itself. "As long as it's clear, it's okay to be complex." "Ignoring complexity in favor of forced simplicity doesn't help anybody."

Example. Research on the ground, prototypes tested in drivers' cars; "localization is not translation".
"Syncing", "label write queued" and "undo in flight" are pending screens. Each needs status, duration, and a way out from where you are.
2018 · 2026

Microcopy carries the mental model

A confirmation sheet was cut for schedule, so one button had to serve card payments (charged now) and cash (steps follow). "Continue" or "Pay"? She chose "Pay", because a surprise charge is the worse failure. Eight years later: the ceiling rose with AI, "but the floor, the boring, unglamorous craft of making a control mean one clear thing, hasn't moved." "'Apply Filters' beats a checkmark."

Example. "This is not a perfect flow for a user, but a good flow for a person."
Spoil, Filed and Contamination change meaning with the autonomy rung. Pair each verb with its Gmail consequence.
2019 – 2026

A design system is a product

"A design system can be considered successful when it helps you, more than it hinders you." Be realistic about capacity, phase it, accept shipping imperfect components, and know that "it's not always about consistency; efficiency might become the priority". Her 2026 coaching example sells system work to a CTO as onboarding confusion avoided, components not rebuilt, and "the cheapest it will ever be".

Example. Shipping the Wealthsimple system: visual refresh first, then one-to-one with the designer of each screen.
Riddle's Bits primitives are a design system whether or not they are called one. Give them do-and-don't notes and a status.
2024 – 2026

Problems before solutions; three questions before Figma

Tell your product manager "you would like to be approached with problems and not solutions". Before opening Figma, write down three things: what business problem, what user problem, how success will be measured. If you lack answers, "schedule 15 minutes with your PM". Overlap between design and product "is actually where ideas can flourish", and it needs documented touch points.

Example. Translate for the room: "users typically behave in this way, which is leading to this type of business impact." (machine transcript)
If a Riddle feature has no metric (time to empty Finds, undo rate, Unresolved trend), that is the finding.
2025 · 2026

Four lenses and a strategic narrative

Reframe any request through customer impact, business outcomes, team efficiency ("that's not opportunity cost, that's opportunity creation") and tech debt and timing. Then present as problem context, insight, solution, expected impact: "quantify it where you can, and be directional when you can't." No analytics? Ask the PM, watch support tickets. No time for fifteen interviews? "Talk to three to five."

Example. An onboarding redesign: 45 percent completion, drop-off where "users don't understand why we're asking", an expected 20 to 30 percent lift. (machine transcript)
The case for empty, loading and error states, made her way: the empty Finds view is the daily reward.
2026

Influence has a timing; recommend, do not list

Designers lose on timing, not communication skill: by the Thursday review the decision is half-made. The three moments: the conversation before the meeting; the Slack thread where a logistics question quietly becomes a decision; and "What do you think?", which deserves a recommendation with a thirty-second why, not a list of options. Work must be "legible to people who weren't there when it happened".

Example. Two evidence-backed sentences at the right moment beat a thirty-minute presentation. (paraphrased from a low-fidelity transcript)
The Director should recommend one change with a reason, not present four options.
2018 – 2025

Storytelling as the shape of an argument

Set the context (problem, goals, assumptions, constraints); go beyond the pixels ("A portfolio isn't an art exhibition"; show rejected explorations); include a post-mortem; show work in its real environment. By 2025 she inverts the format: hiring managers spend under twenty seconds, so lead with the problem, the outcome, then the solution, with the full case on request. A section called "five design decisions I'm proud of" beats a 2,000-word case study.

Example. A poll of design leaders: nobody read "all of it".
First run has about twenty seconds: the problem, what Riddle will and will not touch at Observe, then how.
2017 – 2019

Themes, blocks, requirements before decoration

Daily themes (administration Monday, creation Tuesday and Friday, strategy Wednesday, meetings Thursday) with margin for the unexpected; two hours of early focused time "completely changed my output". Her bullet journal lesson is a design lesson: requirements first, a system that "doesn't distract from the goal", then letting go of the expectation that it look good.

Example. Goals get a cue, a calendar slot and a reachable target.
A keyboard triage block that ends with a summary, not ambient notifications that break it.
2026

AI: judgment is scarce; references before generation

"AI has gotten surprisingly good at reasoning through design problems; the part that still falls short is usually the specificity. Without references, it's mostly working from assumptions." She has the model study real shipped screens first. On teams where PMs vibe-code prototypes: "Design's real leverage was never about the screens. It was judgment." Keep a review checkpoint, but "if it's approval theatre, people will route around it." Experiment in "roof shots, not moonshots".

Example. A three-part 2026 series: research turned into "a design brief with concrete decisions" before building.
The approval step must surface judgment (which messages change, is a Never Hidden sender touched), not lint.

Applied to Riddle

Twelve implications for Riddle's flows

  1. 1
    VocabularyEvery verb must mean one clear thing to Gmail. Find, Filed, Spoil and Contamination are "Continue versus Pay" at scale: what they do changes with the rung. Pair each verb with an explicit consequence wherever it is invoked: "Spoil · archives in Gmail" at Act, "Spoil · recorded only" at Observe. Never an icon or colour alone.From: Microcopy carries the mental model.
  2. 2
    StatusTreat sync and write-back as a pending screen with recovery. Her drivers hit "pending" and phoned support. "Syncing", "label write queued" and "undo in flight" each need a visible status, an expected duration, and cancel, retry or undo from the state itself. Show queued Gmail writes in Activity as distinct from completed ones.From: Design for people, not users.
  3. 3
    UnresolvedUnresolved is where clarity beats simplicity. Do not collapse it to a bare list. Show which Meshes fired, which Signal abstained, and what would resolve it. A clear complex screen beats a simple one that hides why nothing happened.From: Design for people, not users.
  4. 4
    DirectorProposals read as "options compared". The problem with message counts, the alternative rules considered and why rejected, the blast radius, a status. Rejected proposals go to a graveyard that stays queryable in Activity.From: Document throughout.
  5. 5
    ProcessFrame the decision before showing pixels in Riddle's own reviews. Open a UI change with the decision being asked, pre-circulate to whoever owns the Action, ask one crisp question, include the rejected variant. "Vague questions get vague answers."From: Pushback is a framing problem.
  6. 6
    VisualRun the Bits primitives as a product with designer-users. Do-and-don't notes, a status per component, and her test: it "helps you, more than it hinders you". Ship an imperfect row primitive to keep five views on one component, and say when efficiency temporarily wins over consistency.From: A design system is a product.
  7. 7
    ProcessThe "big 3" as the acceptance template. Which problem (false-hide anxiety), how it is easier (fewer keystrokes, clearer state), why the team should support it over the alternative. In review, convert "I don't like the sidebar" into "what doesn't work?"From: A design decision must do three jobs.
  8. 8
    MetricsThree questions before building any feature. Business problem, user problem, success metric, written first. Candidates: time from sync to empty Finds, undo rate per disposition, Unresolved volume trend, zero Never Hidden violations. No metric is the finding.From: Problems before solutions.
  9. 9
    PrioritiesArgue for state and polish work through the four lenses. Customer impact (an empty Finds view is the daily reward; a blank one reads as broken), outcomes (trust drives climbing the ladder), efficiency (one state pattern across five views), timing (cheapest before more views exist).From: Four lenses and a strategic narrative.
  10. 10
    DirectorMake approval a review that earns its keep. Surface what needs judgment: which messages change disposition, whether a Never Hidden sender is touched, the undo path. Do not spend attention on what the system can check. Raise autonomy in "roof shots": per Mesh, reversible, not a global switch.From: AI: judgment is scarce.
  11. 11
    ProcessReference shipped products before designing a new view. Collect real screens of comparable flows, turn them into a brief of concrete decisions, then build. The point is the brief, not the tool.From: AI: references before generation.
  12. 12
    OnboardingFirst run is a twenty-second showcase. The problem (inbox overwhelm), the outcome (what Riddle will and will not touch at Observe), then the solution. Say the consequence before asking for the decision. For daily use: a keyboard triage block that ends with a summary.From: Storytelling as argument; Influence has a timing; Themes and blocks.

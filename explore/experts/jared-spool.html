Jared Spool

Forty-five years of watching real people use real software, distilled into a handful of stories a product team never forgets: the $300 million button, the seducible moment, the gap between what a user knows and what a design needs them to know. For Riddle his lesson is blunt: reach a Find before you ask for anything, and let corrections teach.

Major ideas

Fifteen ideas, each from a study he ran

Spool's authority comes from field studies with real users on real sites, from 1997 on. The ideas below are ordered roughly as he published them; each names the article or talk it came from.

2002

The seducible moment

People arrive with a mission and ignore anything off its path. Borrowing "teachable moment" from education, he defines the seducible moment as the point where a person can be enticed toward something else, usually just after they have found what they came for. Sears advertised financing on its home page, where nobody cares; Dell offered it on the product page and in the cart, and got over ten times the requests.

Example. "The difference between the sites is not in the content, but in the design."
Offer a higher autonomy rung right after Finds are emptied or a run of confirmed calls, never in Settings at startup.
2009

The $300 million button

A retailer's checkout began with a login form. First-time buyers resented registering ("I just want to buy something"); repeat buyers could not remember which account they had. Password resets hit 160,000 a day and three quarters of those people never bought. Replacing "Register" with "Continue" raised purchases 45 percent: $15 million in the first month, $300 million in the first year.

Example. "All we did was change a button." The tax of a gate placed before value is invisible until you watch users hit it.
The add-account wizard is Riddle's login form. Everything before the first Find is "Register".
2013

Design is the rendering of intent

"The designer imagines an outcome and puts forth activities to make that outcome real." Everyone who takes part in rendering an intention is a designer. Two US government sites with similar resources: Global Entry intended to "get the service up and running" and rendered a bureaucratic, error-prone sign-up; We The People intended to prove government could match commercial quality, and did. A frustrating experience is often the accurate rendering of a careless intention.

Example. "What if the team had intended that users would get through the sign-up process without ever seeing an error message?"
Write the intent per flow as a testable sentence: "an Observe-mode user never sees a confirmation dialog."
2016 · 2019

The experience vision

A short story of what using the product will be like five years out: far enough that legacy stops being an excuse, near enough that the technology looks like today's. It is written from observed frustrations, not invented features. Once shared, "march towards the flag" is almost the only rule a team needs, because every roadmap item can be judged by whether it moves closer.

Example. Disney executives walked through a cardboard prototype of the MagicBand experience years before it shipped.
One page: a named person's morning with Riddle in 2031. Judge every new view or DSL keyword against it.
2003 – 2005

The scent of information

Building on Peter Pirolli's information-foraging theory, UIE asked users to rate their confidence before and after each click. On sites where people succeeded, confidence rose with every click; where it fell, they failed. Scent is strong when each page is more specific than the last, weak when a link jumps from specific back to general. Search is where people go when links have failed them.

Example. "User happiness is directly tied to whether users accomplish their goals."
An explanation should get more specific at each step: Mesh, then Signal, then disposition, then what reached Gmail.
2004 · 2011

Trigger words

"Trigger words are the words and phrases that trigger a user into clicking." When users' own words for their target appeared on the home page they succeeded 72 percent of the time; when absent, 6 percent. Failed on-site searches are a log of missing trigger words. Behavioural tells: two Backs in a row means lost scent, and "pogo-sticking" between a list and its details is failure, not comparison.

Example. Analog Devices' link-rich redesign beat its minimalist rival.
A Find row needs the user's words: the sender, the rule's name, "reply due", "invoice". Riddle's corrections are its failed-query log.
2016

Beyond the UX tipping point

Markets move from technology to features to experience to commodity. Once shipping is cheap and quality is table stakes, experience is "the last ground to compete on". An organisation crosses the tipping point when it will not ship something that works technically but has a poor experience. Disney delayed a billion-dollar wearable by two and a half years because "the user experience wasn't right".

Example. Three plays got Disney there: immersive exposure, a shared vision, and a culture of continual learning.
The equivalent for Riddle: refuse to ship a rung of autonomy whose consequences the UI cannot show.
2016 – 2019

Five stages of design maturity

Teams, not companies, move from the Dark Ages, through spot UX, UX as a service and embedded UX, to infused UX, where "everybody in the team understands design". Movement is triggered by exposure to users, demonstrated value and frustration with the previous arrangement. "The team's maturity is based on the least mature person."

Example. His advice is not more designers but more shared exposure: bring the least-exposed people to research, then "let them disrupt everything".
A one-person team still has a least-exposed person: the one who wrote the metaphor.
2015 · 2018

Delight versus frustration (Kano)

Basic expectations frustrate when missing but only reach neutral when present. Performance features pay off linearly. Excitement generators are unexpected, often cheap details that delight, and they decay: Flickr's personalised home page and hotel Wi-Fi were once delightful. Reaching neutral is subtractive ("Stop sucking so much"); reaching delight is additive ("Start being delightful").

Example. "But satisfaction is really just the lack of frustration." Zappos shipping early; TripIt parsing a forwarded confirmation.
Sync that never drops a message and Undo on every write are basic. Fix those before Director polish.
2005 · 2008

What makes a design seem intuitive

Interfaces cannot intuit; people do. Each user has a current knowledge point and a target knowledge point; the distance is "the Gap", and the gap is where design happens. A design feels intuitive only when the points coincide, or when the user is trained to bridge the gap without noticing. The moment they notice they are learning, "intuitive" is gone.

Example. A hotel phone needing 8 for an outside line felt intuitive only because a small sign trained guests unobtrusively.
Gmail's words are the current knowledge point; Dig, Mesh, Spoil and Contamination set the target far away.
2009 · 2018

Against borrowed best practices

Design decisions come in five styles: unintended, self, genius, activity-focused, user-focused. A rule borrowed from elsewhere is genius design without the genius, which only works after enough research to be a genius about these users. Avis broke the asterisk convention, marking optional fields instead of required, and was right, because most fields were required.

Example. "When you think about consistency, you're thinking about the product." Current knowledge requires knowing the users.
Internal consistency of the archaeology metaphor is worth less than matching what Gmail users already know.
2017 · 2018

Satisfaction scores considered harmful

NPS "is neither simple nor profound"; its buckets hide movement, it measures stated intention not behaviour, and it is gamed by surveying only people who completed a purchase. Satisfaction is "the food equivalent of edible". His replacements: the follow-up question "What would've made it better?", error-message counts as frustration proxies, and "observations trump inferences".

Example. A Wells Fargo password-reset problem, like the $300 million button, was found by watching.
No rating prompts. Track corrections per hundred classifications, undo rate, and time to an empty Finds list.
2011

Exposure hours

The closest thing to a silver bullet in UIE's research: the hours each team member spends directly watching real people use the design. The best teams had every member watching users at least two hours every six weeks. Reports and highlight reels do not count. Two mechanisms: memory (the user stays in mind) and motivation (watching someone struggle is intolerable).

Example. "Each team member has to be exposed directly to the users themselves."
Two hours every six weeks watching someone triage their own mailbox with Riddle, on their machine.
2018

Experience rot

"Every feature that's squeezed in, in the name of giving your design a competitive edge, has been making your design less competitive." Features added to match rivals are retrofitted into a design not built for them; complexity accumulates; something with fewer features disrupts it. WordPerfect's 1,700 features against Word for DOS's 70.

Example. "It takes strong organizational leadership to keep the team focused on fighting rot from the beginning."
Say no by default to new Mesh DSL constructs and new rail items.
2017 · 2025

Outcome-driven metrics; usable means secure

Replace CSAT and task time with metrics derived from a stated outcome: a binary success checklist for the user's "party moment", progress metrics toward it, and problem-value metrics that price the cost of today's experience. On authentication: "If it's not usable, it's not secure"; lock-outs and unclear rules are experience failures that stop no breaches. Amazon's graduated security matches friction to actual risk.

Example. "Great metrics tell a story of change."
Match confirmation friction to the real risk of each Gmail write, as the autonomy ladder already tries to do.

Applied to Riddle

Twelve implications for Riddle's flows

  1. 1
    OnboardingReach a Find before asking for anything. Do not gate the first sync behind naming the Dig, choosing autonomy or writing a Mesh: sign in, sync, show Finds. Everything else is "Register" and should become "Continue". Observe by default means nothing is asked of Gmail until value is visible.From: The $300 million button; Outcome-driven metrics.
  2. 2
    VocabularyClose the metaphor gap in place, without visible training. A newcomer's current knowledge is Gmail's words: label, archive, spam. Show "Spoil (archived in Gmail)" in rail tooltips and Activity rows until the verb has been used a few times, then drop the parenthetical. Where the metaphor collides with Gmail, keep the Gmail effect visible in the row.From: What makes a design seem intuitive; Against borrowed best practices.
  3. 3
    Daily loopEvery row needs a trigger word. A Find labelled only by type has no scent. Lead with the user's words: the sender, the matched Mesh's name, "reply due", "invoice". Unresolved should name the trigger word Riddle lacked ("no rule mentions this sender"). Corrections and searches are the failed-query log the Director should mine for rule names.From: Trigger words; The scent of information.
  4. 4
    WhyExplanations should raise confidence with each step. Read as an increasingly specific trail: Mesh matched, Signal score, disposition, what was written to Gmail. Never jump from specific back to general, never end in an unexplained verdict. When Riddle cannot explain, say so in the row.From: The scent of information.
  5. 5
    AutonomyAsk for more autonomy at the seducible moment. The mission is triage; the moment is right after it is satisfied. A one-line offer in place: "Riddle got the last 40 Spoil calls right. Let it archive Spoil?" with the rung and the undo guarantee stated, dismissible.From: The seducible moment.
  6. 6
    PrioritiesBasic expectations before delighters. Sync that never silently drops a message, the Unresolved disposition, Undo on every write-back, no error dialogs in the first session. Absent they frustrate; present they earn neutral. Then one or two thoughtful details, expecting them to become expected.From: Delight versus frustration.
  7. 7
    ProcessWrite the intent per flow, then render it. "No message changes disposition without an Activity row." "Correcting a classification takes one keystroke and shows what will change." The autonomy ladder is intent made explicit; render it by showing the current rung on every control that can write to Gmail.From: Design is the rendering of intent.
  8. 8
    RoadmapDraft a five-year experience vision and use it as the filter. A named person's morning with Riddle in 2031, written from observed frustration with Gmail triage today. Anything there to match another mail client is the keep-up game that leads to rot.From: The experience vision; Experience rot.
  9. 9
    CorrectionsCorrection is the core loop; make it cheap and instructive. One keystroke in the row; the fix visibly teaches, with the Director proposing the rule change right there and the trigger words it would add. Pogo-sticking between list and message to check verdicts is a scent failure to design out.From: Trigger words; Five stages of maturity.
  10. 10
    MetricsMeasure outcomes and frustration, never satisfaction. No rating prompt. Corrections per hundred classifications, Unresolved rate, undo rate, time from launch to an empty Finds list, count of error surfaces shown. A local, private stats panel is the "party moment" checklist for "the user trusts Riddle with Spoil".From: Satisfaction scores considered harmful; Outcome-driven metrics.
  11. 11
    ProcessSchedule exposure hours, even as a team of one. Two hours every six weeks watching someone triage their own live mailbox with Riddle, with consent, on their machine. Reports and telemetry do not count. Start with the wizard and the first correction, where the developer's knowledge diverges most from a newcomer's.From: Exposure hours; What makes a design seem intuitive.
  12. 12
    TrustAutonomy must be an honest rendering of intent. Never pre-select a higher rung, never count "no correction" as consent, never hide a write-back inside a batch. Every Gmail write appears in Activity with an undo, and stepping down the ladder is as easy as stepping up.From: Design is the rendering of intent; Satisfaction scores considered harmful.
Honesty note

What we could not confirm

No Spool-authored article on dark patterns or a literal "best practices are wrong" thesis could be found; the argument above is assembled from his decision-styles, genius-design and consistency articles. No study named "as-is links" exists in his archive; the closest are the Lincoln and trigger-word studies. The 7-to-12-word link-length finding and the Ed Chi attribution appear only in secondary sources we did not fetch. "Anyone who renders intent is a designer" is a social post; the article's wording is "everyone who participates in the rendering of their intention is a designer". "Good design, when done well, becomes invisible" has no source.

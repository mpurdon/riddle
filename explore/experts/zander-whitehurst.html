Major ideas

Nine ideas, each demonstrated on a shipped product

His method is constant: pick one screen, name the principle it demonstrates or violates, rebuild it in Figma in under a minute. The ideas below are the principles that recur across the teardowns.

2025

Hick's law: fewer visible choices

"The more choices you give, the longer it takes to decide. Think of it like a restaurant menu. 50 dishes is overwhelming." His Trip.com teardown counts nine header buttons with inconsistent labels, colours and shapes and rebuilds it with three to five, letting hierarchy carry the rest. Commenters, including Vitaly Friedman, added the caveat: hiding revenue-critical features behind a menu trades discoverability for cleanliness.

Example. LinkedIn, June 2025: 4,200 reactions, 137 comments. It is the only classic UX law he demonstrably teaches.
The message page has eight header controls; the Inbox seven filters. Three to five visible, the rest in a palette with a visible hint.
2024 · 2026

Never ask "Are you sure?"

A confirmation exists to let someone check what will happen; a generic question wastes the one moment of attention it has. Use verb plus noun: "Delete file?", "Remove member?", and let the primary button repeat the verb rather than "OK". His 2026 addition: as more UI is generated by AI, "the small details matter more than ever" and the copy is what still needs a human.

Example. "'Are you sure' tells people nothing." "Text is an underestimated feature of interfaces."
"Archive 42 messages in Gmail?" with the button "Archive", and a line saying what Undo can and cannot reverse.
2024 · 2026

Forms: reduce effort, build confidence, keep moving

Revolut's forms "reduce effort, build confidence, and keep you moving": one field per step, sensible defaults, immediate validation, a call to action that changes as the state changes. The pushback on his 2024 sign-up post is as instructive as the rule: placeholders instead of labels create accessibility and legal blind spots; single-field logins break password managers.

Example. Airbnb, Uber, Dovetail and Revolut sign-up teardowns, with a six-principle hand-out.
The wizard already does one decision per step. Keep visible labels, and let the button read "Connect" then "Start observing".
2024 – 2026

Layout discipline: anchors, radius, text styles

The eye reads alignment before content. Stacked centred text "creates too many alignment anchors"; every distinct left edge, centre line or radius is a thing the reader must reconcile. "Inner radius + gap = outer radius" makes nested corners read as one object. Never detach text styles: a system is a set of named decisions tools can enforce.

Example. "Stop centring text in your UI" (29,000 likes); "Stop guessing your corner radius"; the Figma UI Masterclass modules on spacing systems and grids.
One alignment anchor per column in every list; hierarchy by weight and size, not colour alone.
2022 – 2025

Motion with intent

Two years of prototyping tutorials (radio buttons, carousels, the Dynamic Island, a Disney-style loader) resolve into one rule: motion's job is to confirm that something happened and where it went. "Test motion with intent: evaluate behavior and transitions, not final motion quality." He tunes duration and Bézier curves on Monzo's freeze-card interaction and judges it by how the state change feels.

Example. Figma Make post, December 2025.
A resolved Find leaves the list with a short, purposeful motion and a toast that names the verb and count. Respect reduced motion.
2022 – 2026

Design systems: tokens, variables, then code

Spacing systems in week three of the bootcamp, then components and documentation. Variables for light and dark mode and for breakpoints. The 2026 bootcamp extends the ladder into code: primitive and semantic tokens, agentic components via Claude Code and the Figma MCP, then React tokens and components. The through-line: a system is a set of named decisions so designers stop re-deciding them.

Example. "Switch designs from light to dark mode in Figma, supafast!" (2023); Figma's blog called him a "supafast-aficionado".
Riddle's tokens are primitives. A semantic layer and derived radii would let density variants change in one place.
2024 · 2025

Teardowns and a library organised by flow

He teaches almost entirely by dissecting shipped products: Trip.com, Revolut, Airbnb, Uber, Monzo, Apple, ChatGPT. In 2025 Memorisely published a free UI inspiration library of complete Figma files "organized by product flow", including onboarding, checkout and empty states, arguing that "access to great UI patterns should be open".

Example. The library post is the only place his material names empty states and onboarding as topics.
Treat each empty state as part of onboarding: an empty Unresolved explains that Riddle asks here when it cannot decide.
2020 – 2026

Learn by doing, inside your files

"These sessions aren't lectures. They're live, inside-your-files, figure-it-out-together moments." One topic, one month. "Design experts do not exist" was his Friends of Figma Sydney talk; "perfect portfolios don't exist" his advice to juniors. He builds in public: the platform's private beta reported 113 founding members in its first week, difficulties included.

Example. The Memorisely YouTube channel's description: "Teaching you by doing, not just by talking."
Teach the Mesh DSL by live matching in the user's own mail, not from a syntax box.
2025 · 2026

AI for momentum, not answers; designers who build

Five legitimate uses of AI prototyping: break blocks, explore direction faster, start conversations, test motion, validate through feel. The warning: "Make rewards design precision not relentless prompts." Designers keep interaction design, the system and edge-case thinking; AI takes repetitive component generation. "The role of the designer is no longer to just imagine a product. It's to build it." Validate before building: start with a waitlist.

Example. Monthly 2026 workshops on Claude Code with the Figma MCP and Figma Make; a 12-file set of AI prototyping skills.
Prototype the two risky flows (rung promotion, proposal approval) with real tokens and watch someone use them before building.

Applied to Riddle

Eleven implications for Riddle's flows

  1. 1
    TrustConfirmations for Gmail writes use verb plus noun with a count. "Archive 42 messages in Gmail?", "Remove work@example.com?" with the primary button repeating the verb. Because Riddle has Undo, the dialog also says what Undo covers ("restores labels; Gmail's own trash timer still applies").From: Never ask "Are you sure?"
  2. 2
    Daily loopCap visible row actions in Finds at three to five. Show the most-used dispositions plus one "More"; route the rest through the keyboard and a palette. Hidden is not gone: the palette needs a visible hint.From: Hick's law.
  3. 3
    AutonomyPresent the ladder as one recommended next rung, not four peers. On the account card: the current rung, the single next rung with its consequence in one line, and a link to the full ladder.From: Hick's law.
  4. 4
    DirectorProposals state their effect in verb-plus-noun copy. "Route newsletters from sender X to Spoil: affects 118 messages, 3 currently in Finds", with the button "Apply rule", not "Approve", and the DSL diff under a disclosure.From: Never ask "Are you sure?"; AI for momentum.
  5. 5
    OnboardingThe wizard: one decision per screen, persistent labels, an evolving call to action. Visible labels rather than placeholders, a progress indicator, and a button that reads "Connect" then "Start observing". OAuth in the system browser so password managers work.From: Forms that reduce effort.
  6. 6
    VisualOne alignment anchor per column in the triage lists. Left-align every cell; never centre the disposition badge or counts. Hierarchy by weight and size: subject, sender, snippet, reason.From: Layout discipline.
  7. 7
    VisualDerive nested radii and never detach text styles. Radius, spacing and type tokens defined once; rows inside cards and badges inside rows use inner = outer minus gap. Dark mode as a semantic-token switch.From: Layout discipline; Design systems.
  8. 8
    Daily loopMicro-feedback after every disposition, timed on the real list. The row leaves with a short purposeful motion; the toast reads "Filed · Undo" with the count. Judge by whether people can tell what happened and where it went. Never animate for decoration.From: Motion with intent.
  9. 9
    VocabularyEvery view has a designed empty state that says what the view means. Empty Unresolved explains Riddle asks here when it cannot decide; empty Meshes offers a starter rule or the Director; empty Spoil reassures. Empty states are onboarding, not errors.From: Teardowns and a library organised by flow.
  10. 10
    MeshesTeach the DSL by doing, with live results. The editor shows "this rule currently matches N messages" with the first few listed; the Director shows the rule it would write so people learn the grammar from examples. The help sheet stays short.From: Learn by doing, inside your files.
  11. 11
    ProcessShip the minimum triage loop; prototype the two risky flows first; make Unresolved read as a feature. Sync, classify, Finds, undo, then iterate in public. Watch someone promote an account from Suggest to Act in a prototype with real tokens before building it. Surface the Unresolved count in the rail as honesty made visible.From: Learn by doing; AI for momentum.
Honesty note

What we could not confirm

Hick's law is the only UX law he demonstrably teaches. Fitts's law appears only in a commenter's critique of his sign-up post; Jakob's, Miller's, Doherty, Zeigarnik, Von Restorff, peak-end and Tesler's laws were not found in any fetched material, so the brief's premise that he popularises the laws of UX is unproven beyond Hick's. No standalone content on empty states, onboarding sequences or accessibility contrast and target sizes was found. The "UX Dad" nickname, the TikTok follower count, and the Eedi, NYU and Oxford degree details rest on secondary pages or were unreadable. The "1 million designers" figure comes from event billing; Memorisely's own site says 25,000 trained.

Major ideas

Twelve ideas, each from a specific video

Every quotation comes from YouTube's automatic captions, checked against the transcript, so punctuation is ours. Kole's method is the same in every video: take a real screen, name what is wrong, rebuild it, and say why.

June 2026

Let the data drive the form

"Designing a dashboard isn't that hard, but orchestrating one is." The first tell of an inexperienced dashboard is a table where nothing is driving the form. Enumerated values become chips because "there's only a set number of departments"; numbers are right-aligned "so the digits align by place value"; long text truncates; inactive rows are shaded. Time-delineated data "probably shouldn't even be in a table": an activity log is a timeline, and a chart rolls up the time dimension so nobody hunts through a timestamp column.

Example. "Dashboard colors shouldn't just be sprinkled in to look nice. It should come from the data itself." The red icon in a chip draws the eye because the action is urgent; the avatar is there because the eye associates who faster than reading a name.
Dispositions and Signal names are enumerations: chips. Counts and times: right-aligned. Activity is a timeline, not a table.
June 2026

Progressive disclosure and the spectrum of explicitness

A second kind of hierarchy: "what you show and what you hide". A share feature that is not frequent enough to live beside the table goes in a popover "so we don't have to rip the user to an entirely different page"; inside, the primary action is at the top and the dangerous one appears on hover with a tooltip. Kole names the scale: "the spectrum of explicitness, where a global, always visible share button is quite high, whereas an icon to copy a cell on hover is much lower." Onboarding is the same idea over time.

Example. "You're not hiding functionality, you're sequencing it so that the user is never overwhelmed." A tooltip on the most important action, then a second, then a corner checklist; never "the entire product in a modal with six bullet points".
File and Find stay visible; Spoil, Escalate and Contamination reveal on hover and focus. First run is a sequence, not a modal.
June 2026 · March 2026

The UI you cannot see

"UI is as much about what you can see as what you can't see." In a dense table, extra function must come from parts of the UI that are not immediately visible: a copy chip over a cell, a comment indicator, drawers, states. "Here's all the UI that you can see and then this is all of the UI that you can't immediately see but is still there. And it's not a small amount." Tooltips are the thing "missing pretty much unequivocally on all beginner dashboards". Every button needs four states at least; a copy button with states still "doesn't know that we've copied anything" until a chip slides up.

Example. Kole flags this as the concept "AI has a hard time wrapping its figurative head around". On the keyboard: "If you don't see a change, you assume that something went wrong."
A tooltip on every rail and row icon; default, hover, pressed, disabled and loading on every disposition control; a toast for every Gmail write.
September 2026

Scannability: edges, differentiation, show don't tell

Built from a receipt: text hard left, prices hard right, and "the only center things are the parts you're not really meant to read at all." In compact UI "everything in here borders on at least two or more edges"; full-width dividers break it. Density is not the problem: "there's too much undifferentiated content", and with avatars and grouping "the density is now working for us". Explaining harder makes a screen "harder to read in order to make it easier to understand, which is a trade-off you almost never want." Cards are a crutch: one column edge does what the card edge did.

Example. "Nobody reads a screen. You land somewhere with a question already in your head and you hunt until you find the answer." Chips all in a default colour are "like having six checkboxes that are blue when they're off."
Rows as receipts: sender and subject hard left, time and count hard right, no row cards, neutral states grey so Unresolved finds the eye.
August 2025

Dashboard anatomy: spine, one job, four components

The sidebar is "the spine of your product": a recognisable icon and a short title per link so it can collapse and carry badges; settings and help "down to the bottom"; an active state; "Navigation is all about reducing cognitive load." The main area "reflects what is most important to the user" and its top row is reserved for page actions. "This dashboard feels like someone emptied a drawer onto the screen." Lists are stacked, not bordered, which "also makes it easier to add an empty state". Only four components exist: lists and tables, cards, inputs, tabs.

Example. The container ladder: a popover when the context is simple and non-blocking; a modal when complex but same page, followed by a toast; a new page with a breadcrumb for permanent contexts. "On Gmail, when I delete an email, it disappears instantly."
Explain a decision in a popover; edit a Mesh in a modal then a toast; open a message on a page. Dispositions are optimistic; Undo restores.
January 2026 · September 2025

Colour for products: four layers, double the distance in the dark

The landing-page rule of 60-30-10 fails in software. Layer one is the neutral foundation: "four layers of backgrounds, at least one stroke, often two, and then about three text variants"; the sidebar is "a slightly darker anchor" (Mercury adds 2 percent blue); card edges use "roughly 85% white", not a black stroke; "the more important a button is, the darker it is." Layer two is one accent as a ramp (500 primary, 700 hover). Dark mode is not inversion: "dark colors look more similar, so they require more distance… a good rule of thumb is double the distance", and surfaces get lighter as they elevate. Layer three is semantic: destructive is red, "pretty much a design sin" otherwise.

Example. "Icons need no color… Color should be reserved for communicating status." "Getting comfortable with using more gray and less black and white is really what differentiates mediocre designers from professional ones."
Paper, sand and clay as the four neutral layers; ochre as the one accent ramp; moss and rust semantic only; wider steps in dark mode.
June 2025 · March 2025

The beginner-mistakes checklist

A recipe app redesigned one mistake at a time. Flow first: six preset options "but no search bar" and "no skip button either… users, they'll feel it instantly"; the most commonly missed elements are "navigation links, hidden states, and dynamic interactions". Effects: lighten and blur shadows or remove them, "less visual noise equals a better design". One small-component radius; "styles for colors, variables for measurements, and components for UI elements"; one icon library with tooltips for obscure icons; drop redundant arrows and strokes; a pressed state so "the next screen is coming".

Example. The cheat codes: nested radii ("take the outer corner radius and subtract the distance between the two corners"), "lose the lines" in favour of spacing or alternating tints, dark-mode depth by lightening each layer 4 to 6 points.
A pass over every page: skip, search and back present; one radius; one icon set; no redundant strokes; pressed states; charts with axes.
July 2025 · June 2025

Product thinking: states, flows, a system you can trust

Decorating a room versus designing "the entire house: the flow, the function, the wiring, and how it all connects". Linear is the model: "The states, the flows, and the system make it feel effortless." "Most designers just design the happy path… real apps need to handle all the states users actually experience." Think in sequences: how did the user get here and what do they need next; Notion's new team space prompts for members "with the option to skip". System is trust: a macOS dialog "has saved me so many times because the primary color is the non-destructive action and I trust that it's always that way."

Example. "You don't break the system by accident. You break it with intention." To learn: annotate real screens, and "pick a design you like and don't redesign it. Literally copy it."
Chain the Director flow as tiny decisions with a skip; keep the primary colour always on the non-destructive action.
December 2025

An app that belongs on your computer

Designing the macOS app that became Stash: "The best UX an app can have is feeling like it belongs on your computer", so "the app shouldn't be a destination, but a system exactly where you need it." A Spotlight-style ⌘⇧S panel appears, does its job, collapses into a toast, and saves in the background: optimistic UI, "moving the email to trash before it's actually deleted off the server". Apple's utilities share one layout: global actions at the top, navigation in the sidebar, content in the centre, the traffic lights integrated "just like any other element", the top ~50 px kept draggable.

Example. "What is the minimum UI needed to really let that content shine?" Desktop apps still need onboarding: a modal whose "way to close it is to execute the shortcut", plus a cheat sheet, because shortcuts are "easy to forget".
Keep the drag strip free of actions; close the first-run tip by pressing the key it teaches; a floating ⌘K that gets out of the way.
June 2024 · March 2026

Dense typography: one font, a small ratio, nothing over 24 px

"One font is entirely acceptable… you can have two and that's it." Weight and colour are interchangeable levers, so hierarchy uses both; body at 100 percent opacity, secondary text "between 40 to 70%"; "you should literally be able to list the hierarchy of all the text elements." The golden ratio grows too fast for interfaces, so use its square root, or its cube root "if you're designing interfaces with many many different font styles like a dashboard UI". For dashboards "you don't normally have text sizes larger than 24 pixels because of the increase in information density"; icons sized to the line height; the 4-point grid "because you can always split things in half".

Example. Line height around 150 percent for paragraphs, 110 to 130 for headings; tighten large headings by 2 to 3 percent; buttons padded at double the height for the width.
A cube-root-of-phi scale for Riddle's dense views, hierarchy by weight and opacity, and a written hierarchy list for the Find row.
June 2024 · May 2026

The redesign method: assess, toss, tier-rank, then earn the details

On a finance dashboard, "our first order of business is to do an assessment of the hierarchy and probably end up tossing a few elements": duplicate search bars, a label that restates the page, twelve cards merged or cut. Then a tier list: "the more important information should be higher and farther left." The four-level rubric grades the result: level one "is just a product of someone not making any design decisions whatsoever"; at level three "designers stop being UI designers and start becoming product designers"; level four moves copy "from what it does to how it helps".

Example. "The difference between good and great is almost never one big thing." For clients "the goal isn't to wow them. It's to build confidence in the product." "Don't describe, show."
Autonomy copy at level four: "Act: Riddle files newsletters so you never see them", not "Act: grants write access".
February 2026 · August 2026

Vibe-coded UI: AI handles logic, not colour, layout or the invisible parts

Redesigning a real AI-built URL shortener: "Get rid of the emojis"; "never let an AI choose any of your colors. They always go for bright colors that don't really work well together"; the same four KPI cards repeated three times in a small app; "Never let an AI choose your layout either"; a card that "doesn't do anything… a pattern you're going to see a lot with AI". The balance: "AI sets up logic well if you tell it to", and layout is where a human's hour buys the most. Kole's own workflow uses AI for first passes and mock-ups, not for thoughtful UI.

Example. A submitted site with no images anywhere, "a telltale sign that this was vibe coded and leads to a robotic-feeling website".
The Director never picks colours, emojis or layout. It emits DSL and verb-plus-object copy rendered by Riddle's components; each count appears once.

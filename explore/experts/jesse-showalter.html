Major ideas

Fifteen ideas, from the fundamentals to the critiques

Video quotations come from YouTube's automatic captions, so punctuation is ours; article quotations are his written text.

2022

C.R.A.P. is the whole foundation

Contrast organises, establishes hierarchy and gives a focal point. Repetition increases consistency and "users' ability to learn your interface"; without it a page "looks like a yard sale". Alignment "brings order to chaos"; he draws the invisible axes elements share. Proximity makes elements seem related: "does this headline belong to this text?" Hierarchy is added by subtraction and grouping, not decoration.

Example. A bike-shop layout: a 24 px label becomes a 54 px semibold headline, body copy pulled to its heading, "about 100 times better".
Riddle's row cards and borders can go; grouping and alignment carry the hierarchy.
2021 · 2024

"If everything is on fire, nothing is on fire"

One focal point per screen. A junior login reuses the primary pink "in every single area of the form"; a register button "equally weighted, equally sized" to sign-in makes the user "stop and guess". The fix list is small: one primary call to action, secondary actions demoted to text links, labels visibly different from placeholders, a reading order of purpose, inputs, escape hatch.

Example. "The most important thing on this card is the price." Dribbble: "Make sure that you give prominence to the most important elements."
A row with Approve and Dismiss at equal weight is the register-versus-sign-in mistake.
2022 · 2023

Colour is a budget: 60-30-10

Sixty percent neutral base, thirty percent a primary or brand colour, ten percent for the call to action, "used sparingly". It holds in dark variants and is "just a guideline" to break knowingly. System colours are reserved: red and bright green for error and success, "not being used correctly" on a red logout. Never pure black on pure white.

Example. Over-colouring is "where designs start to look cheap, they start to look immature."
Paper and sand as the sixty; clay and moss for structure; one accent for the single primary action; rust only for Contamination and errors.
2024

Spacing from the outside in

"Choose, adopt and adhere to some sort of spacing system": an 8 px grid for spacing, radii and type, so he can "make decisions quickly and ask less questions". At least 6 px between elements. Then five nested groups with decreasing spacing: wrapper 24 to 30, sections 32, title to content 24, inside cards 16, between buttons and tags 8, regular elements 6. Start with too much white space and work back.

Example. His first move on any cluttered card: "remove the excess borders and boxes" and use subtle neutral backgrounds instead.
A compact ladder for dense lists: gutter 16, section 24, heading-to-list 16, row 8, chip gap 4 to 6.
2020 · 2021

Typography: one family, a scale, skipped weights

Four rules: justify left; one family or two categories; skip a weight for contrast (light to medium, never light to book) because "typography is really all about contrast"; choose a type scale "and stick with it for your entire project". Ten to fourteen words per line. And a rule that matters for dense UI: "as your typography size decreases your line height must increase."

Example. An 18 px base, line height at 1.618, headings by the same ratio, weight added where size alone fails.
Riddle's 10 to 11 px chips and metadata need proportionally more leading, not less.
2024

Interface design is not web design; tools may be boring

A junior dashboard fails because "the emphasis is being placed on the wrong thing": a giant top bar and a muted sidebar say the logo matters and the content does not. "We are building tools and tools should be simple, easy, straightforward and sometimes even a little bit more boring." Strip brand colour ("it's not about the brand while you're in here"), reusable headlines, no boxes around everything, global controls at the top and local filters in context.

Example. "I don't want to know what brand of the hammer is, I just want to start using the hammer."
The Summary page as a boring dashboard: counts in whitespace, colour only on the number that needs attention.
2017 · 2018

UI is not UX: skeleton and skin

UX is "a little bit more analytical", UI "the look and feel and the interaction". "A UX Designer is gonna pass off the skeleton, and the UI Designer is gonna put on the skin." Seven foundations a product must meet: useful, usable, desirable, learnable, memorable, accessible, credible. UX copy "tries to get things done as quickly and efficiently as possible"; his example replaces "form error" with what the field needs.

Example. Testing is "watching them stumble and fumble and bumble."
The archaeology metaphor is skin. Test whether a newcomer can predict what Spoil contains before reading help.
2019 – 2022

The Rework checklist

On an admin panel: "too many boxes inside of boxes inside of boxes", removed first "so that I can look at the information"; widen cramped inputs; move controls so they are "contextual to the information". On cards: shadow opacity under ten percent, subtle radius, consistent padding, a call to action that says it goes somewhere. On a daily-use app: "image upon image upon image, giving me anxiety"; keep it neutral and "let the neutrality do the calming".

Example. Mismatched shapes read as "like you didn't have intentionality"; equal-sized avatars compete for the eye.
Run the checklist on each page: boxes in boxes, cramped inputs, detached controls, mismatched radii, equal-weight competitors.
2019 – 2026

Components, tokens and a plain-English rules page

"Have as few components as possible and with as much variation as we can get." By 2022 the system is the deliverable: "the power lies in the system and the governance and the rule sets and the documentation." Primitive tokens "void of any semantic meaning" feed semantic tokens so dark mode "just jumps right over". In 2026: "design systems are being written for AI to read"; "if it's more than six, you don't have a system, you have a folder."

Example. His exercise: "write one page of plain English rules that a stranger could follow."
A one-page design.md the Director's model can follow, and a variant audit of Button, Chip and Row.
2017 – 2023

Paper, flows, then a ten-minute test

Wireframe on paper first, "8 to 10 different thumbnail sketches" with large changes, grey-box them so clients see structure, "sleep on it". User flows need an entry, "steps to completion", a decision diamond ("one of those non-negotiables") and labels like "found / not found". Testing: state a hypothesis, give fewer screens so testers "hyper focus", add an instructions screen with "a big ugly button" and an exit form.

Example. "Users are going to be able to find the place where they can write a review."
Two hypotheses for Riddle: a new user reaches Finds without help; a user finds where to approve a proposal and knows its rung.
2022 – 2026

"If you can't make it move, you can't defend it"

Static screens under-communicate: "we shouldn't have to ask people to use their imagination." Screen-to-screen prototyping is "a necessary evil, tedious, hard to maintain"; by 2025 he builds app prototypes that open at a staging link and become "a single source of truth for functionality and interactivity". "Static screens are not a deliverable anymore." And: "if you can only prompt but you can't evaluate what comes back, you're just shipping bad work faster."

Example. Live text input is the thing a Figma prototype cannot fake.
Keyboard behaviour is exactly what static screens force people to imagine. Prototype the triage loop, not screens of it.
2022 · 2026

Defending decisions and giving critique

"A design decision that you can't defend is not a decision, it's a preference." "The most common failure in design isn't bad work, it's good work that never ships." Lead with the decision, the reason and what you gave up: sixty seconds, then the screens. Taste: generate five versions, pick one, write 150 words on why the other four are wrong. Feedback: diagnostic questions first, be specific, show examples, say "we", consolidate by topic.

Example. Strategy in one line: "This exists so that [specific person] can [specific outcome]."
"This exists so that a person triaging 300 mails can trust that nothing important is hidden."
2025 · 2026

Client lessons: subtraction, governance, outcomes

"Don't overdesign things, don't crowd things, but make space for the product." Consistency via a shared token set so "a button is a button no matter where you place it". Lock what can break so clients "can create new pages and scale" without damage. Strategy is often subtraction: clients wanted features "slammed into the product when the right call was cutting". "Clients pay for confidence, not just deliverables."

Example. Handoff annotated "with intent, not just measurements"; "document the why behind decisions".
Say no to a seventh rail item; annotate the Action layer's intent, not only its arguments.
2026

The 90% trap

AI raises surface quality so "90% reads as 'almost there'", but in product design ninety percent is "load-bearing problems hidden under a nice Dribbble shot", and "someone hitting 90% for the first time doesn't know what they're not seeing." The hidden ten: token discipline, component governance, true accessibility, "state coverage: empty, loading, error, and edge cases", content architecture, documentation. A button is "a promise of state change, network calls, validation rules, and accessibility needs".

Example. "If your job can be fully described in a checklist, AI is already learning it."
A screenshot-perfect Finds page with no first-sync, expired-auth or Observe state is the trap.
2022

The four UX laws he actually uses

Fitts: targets large enough to tap, with space between, placed where they are accessible. Hick: more choices "cognitively overload your users"; break flows into steps, highlight the option you want, disclose progressively. Jakob: "don't make your users learn new mental models", and "if you have to stray, just don't stray too far." Miller: seven plus or minus two, and "if you have less options go with less."

Example. "Always provide the user feedback, think of your UX designs more like a conversation."
Keep Gmail's keys (j, k, e, /) and mental models rather than inventing new ones.

Applied to Riddle

Thirteen implications for Riddle's flows

  1. 1
    VisualMake the rail and header disappear. Riddle is a tool, not a brand moment. Keep chrome in paper and sand neutrals with modest weight; the list is the focal point. If a screenshot's most saturated pixels are in the rail, that is the "giant muted sidebar" failure.From: Interface design is not web design.
  2. 2
    VisualBudget the palette 60-30-10 and reserve the system colours. Paper and sand as base; clay and moss for structure; one accent for the single primary action per view. Rust only for Contamination and errors, moss only for success and Filed, never decoratively, so disposition chips keep meaning.From: Colour is a budget.
  3. 3
    Daily loopOne prominence carrier per row. Decide whether sender or subject carries weight; demote reason, confidence, account and time with opacity and weight rather than colour. Labels read differently from values. One primary action, the rest text.From: If everything is on fire.
  4. 4
    VisualDensity needs a spacing ladder, not less spacing. 8 pt tokens and a compact outside-in ladder: gutter 16, section 24, heading-to-list 16, row 8, chip gap 4 to 6, never under 6. Strip row cards and borders; a subtle neutral hover and selection surface instead.From: Spacing from the outside in.
  5. 5
    VisualOne typeface, one scale, longer leading in small sizes. A single UI sans plus a mono for the Mesh DSL, a 13 to 14 px base, a fixed ratio, skipped weights. Metadata at 11 to 12 px gets more line height. Director chat and bodies at a 10 to 14 word measure.From: Typography.
  6. 6
    SummaryThe Summary page as a boring dashboard. Clear the chrome; counts in whitespace-separated sections with repeated headline styles; no nested boxes; colour only on the number that needs attention; global controls at the top, per-account filters in context.From: Tools may be boring; C.R.A.P.
  7. 7
    VisualPrimitive to semantic tokens, and a plain-English rules page. Map sand and rust primitives to surface.default, status.contamination, status.spoil, autonomy.trusted. Audit Button, Chip and Row variants: more than six is a folder. Write one page of rules a stranger, or the Director's model, can follow.From: Components, tokens and a rules page.
  8. 8
    ProcessTest the two risky flows with ten-minute prototypes. "A new user can add an account and reach Finds without help"; "a user can find where to approve a proposal and understand its rung". Few screens, entry and exit screens, keyboard-driven.From: Paper, flows, then a ten-minute test; If you can't make it move.
  9. 9
    PrioritiesDesign the states before the polish. Empty Finds, first sync, expired Gmail auth, judge unavailable, undo confirmations in Activity, and a visible Observe state. This is his hidden ten percent.From: The 90% trap.
  10. 10
    KeyboardHick, Miller and Jakob for the row action set. Five to seven visible actions at most, Mesh matches and Signal explanations behind a keystroke, and Gmail's mental models (j, k, e, /) rather than new ones.From: The four UX laws he uses.
  11. 11
    ProcessEvery UI decision gets a defensible sentence. "This exists so that a person triaging 300 mails can trust nothing important is hidden." Lead reviews with decision, reason and trade-off before screenshots. For contested rows, five variants and 150 words on why four are wrong.From: Defending decisions.
  12. 12
    CritiqueRun the Rework checklist on each page. Boxes in boxes, cramped inputs, controls detached from what they act on, mismatched radii, equal-weight competitors, anything present "for design's sake". Start critique with diagnostic questions; say "we".From: The Rework checklist; Giving critique.

    VocabularyThe metaphor is skin; validate the skeleton. Pair each term with a plain subtitle on first use, and test whether a newcomer can predict what Spoil contains without the help sheet.From: UI is not UX.
